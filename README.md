# The Budapest Transmission: Rátz, Polanyi, Wigner, von Neumann, and the Deep Structure of Machine-Resistant Knowledge

> "Man's capacity to think is his most outstanding attribute."
> — Eugene Wigner, speaking of Michael Polanyi (as quoted in Structural Chemistry, 2016)

> "Polanyi decisively marked Wigner's thinking, not just about physics, but also about philosophy and politics."
> — Abraham Pais, historian of physics (as quoted in Structural Chemistry, 2016)

> "The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve."
> — Eugene Wigner, "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences," 1960

> "All these difficulties are but consequences of our refusal to see that mathematics cannot be defined without acknowledging its most obvious feature: namely, that it is interesting."
> — Michael Polanyi, *Personal Knowledge*, 1958 — cited by Wigner in the footnotes of the above

## A Correction, and What the Correction Reveals

The claim that Wigner was von Neumann's teacher is wrong in the narrow sense and illuminating in the broader one. The true structure of the lineage is more intricate, and more significant, than a simple teacher-student chain.

**The correct genealogy:**

László Rátz, mathematics teacher at the Budapest-Fasori Evangélikus Gimnázium, taught both Eugene Wigner (1915–1919) and John von Neumann in the same decade (Rátz, active 1890–1925; Wikipedia; MacTutor). They were co-students of the same extraordinary pedagogue, not teacher and student to each other. One cannot find a Neumann Street or a Wigner Street in Budapest, but there is a Rátz László Street (Europhysics News, 1999).

Michael Polanyi — the philosopher of tacit knowledge who opens the *Attentional Arbitrage* companion README with "We can know more than we can tell" — was Eugene Wigner's doctoral supervisor at the Technische Hochschule Berlin. Wigner's 1925 dissertation, *Bildung und Zerfall von Molekülen* ("Formation and Decay of Molecules"), was supervised by Polanyi at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physical Chemistry, where Polanyi was then a research chemist (MacTutor; American Mathematical Society Notices, 1995). Wigner later wrote that Polanyi was, after Rátz, his greatest teacher, and that "his finest gift was to encourage my work in physics, and this he did with all of his very great heart. In all my life, I have never known anyone who used encouragement as skillfully as Polanyi. He was truly an artist of praise" (National Academies Biographical Memoir, 1998).

The full lineage is therefore:

```
László Rátz (pedagogy)
        │
        ├──► Eugene Wigner ──► Michael Polanyi (PhD mentorship, then lifelong dialogue)
        │
        └──► John von Neumann
```

And across the horizontal axis:

```
Michael Polanyi (tacit knowledge) ◄──────────────► Eugene Wigner (quantum physics)
                                        [decade-long correspondence, 1961–1971]
                                        [Wigner cites Polanyi in 1960 paper]

Eugene Wigner (Nobel 1963) ◄──────────────────────► John von Neumann
                                        [co-students; Princeton IAS colleagues]
```

This is not a chain. It is a web. And the web has a direct bearing on the epistemological claims at the center of the *Attentional Arbitrage* framework — because what binds these four figures together is precisely the question the framework is trying to answer: what is the nature of knowledge that machines cannot hold?

## I. László Rátz and the Pedagogy of the Tacit

Rátz (1863–1930) is now a footnote in the biographies of two of the twentieth century's most consequential thinkers. He deserves more than that. Between 1912 and 1921, the Fasori Gymnasium produced Eugene Wigner, John von Neumann, Edward Teller, Alfred Haar, and John Harsányi — a concentration of scientific talent without precedent in the history of secondary education (Europhysics News, 1999). Theodore Herzl, founder of Zionism, had also graduated from the same institution decades earlier.

What Rátz did has direct relevance to the pedagogical questions raised in the *Attentional Arbitrage* companion documents. The Fasori principle, as reconstructed by educational historians, was that students were presented with examples before rules — required to encounter the problem on its own terms before being given the framework to solve it (Sarah Constantin, "Hungarian Mathematics Education," 2014). Rátz founded and edited KoMaL, the Hungarian high school mathematics journal, still in print, which presented open problems for student submission rather than closed exercises for mechanical solution. He gave private lessons to von Neumann. He gave Wigner books. He cultivated the personal relationship between student and domain that constitutes, in Polanyi's later vocabulary, the indwelling through which tacit knowing is first formed.

In the language of Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000), Rátz was an autonomy-supportive educator in an environment of high-challenge, low-external-evaluation — the opposite of the prestige competition architecture the *Attentional Arbitrage* framework identifies as motivationally destructive. The Fasori graduates did not emerge having been optimized for metrics. They emerged having been given, as Wigner put it, "encouragement" — the motivational substrate on which genuine capability forms.

## II. Michael Polanyi: From Physical Chemistry to the Philosophy of Machine Limits

Michael Polanyi (1891–1976) is cited in the *Attentional Arbitrage* companion as the philosopher of tacit knowledge. What goes unmentioned there is that he arrived at philosophy from science — from the same Hungarian Jewish intellectual milieu as Wigner and von Neumann, from the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute in Berlin, from collaboration with Einstein, Planck, and von Laue, and from a career in physical chemistry that produced foundational work in adsorption theory, X-ray crystallography, and chemical reaction rates before he pivoted to epistemology (Springer Nature, *Structural Chemistry*, 2016).

Polanyi was "one of the last polymaths" (Springer, 2016). His philosophical turn was not a retreat from science but an interrogation of it from within: having practiced science at the highest level, he became concerned with what science actually is, as opposed to what the positivist account of it claimed. *Personal Knowledge* (1958) was the result — an argument that all knowledge, including scientific knowledge, is grounded in the personal, embodied, apprentice-acquired tacit knowing of the practitioner, and that no account of knowledge that omits this personal dimension is coherent. *The Tacit Dimension* (1966) condensed that argument into its famous single line.

The significance for machine intelligence is now axiomatic in the philosophy of AI: what Polanyi identified as the ineliminable personal dimension of knowing is structurally unavailable to any system that processes codified information. Collins's subsequent taxonomy (2010) — relational, somatic, and collective tacit knowledge — formalizes Polanyi's claim into the terms under which AI capability limits can be precisely specified. The jagged frontier (Kapoor et al., 2026) is the empirical surface of a philosophical claim Polanyi made in 1958.

What is less often noted is that Polanyi knew this directly. His student Eugene Wigner, who had by 1960 won the Nobel Prize and was working on the foundations of quantum mechanics, was grappling with precisely the question of what conscious knowing adds to a physical description of the world. Their correspondence from 1961 to 1971, analyzed in *Physics in Perspective* (Springer, 2011), was an extended dialogue on tacit knowledge and the quantum measurement problem — the question of what an observer contributes to a quantum measurement that a machine register cannot. They never resolved their differences. But their disagreement was productive: it traced the exact boundary between what formal systems can capture and what requires a knower.

## III. Eugene Wigner: The Physicist Who Posed the Question the Framework Answers

Wigner won the Nobel Prize in 1963 for his contributions to the theory of the atomic nucleus and elementary particles, specifically for his discovery and application of symmetry principles — a program that Polanyi's assistantship had originally prompted by directing Wigner toward group theory (Encyclopedia.com; MacTutor). But two of Wigner's contributions are more directly relevant to the *Attentional Arbitrage* framework than his Nobel work.

**"The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences" (1960)** is Wigner's most widely cited philosophical paper. It poses a puzzle that has never been resolved: why do mathematical structures developed with no reference to the physical world turn out to describe that world with uncanny precision? Wigner cites Polanyi's *Personal Knowledge* directly in a footnote, aligning his own wonder at mathematical applicability with Polanyi's account of tacit knowing. The paper is not merely a curious observation about physics; it is a statement about the limits of any account of knowledge that reduces it to formal manipulation. Mathematics "works" in the natural sciences not because the world is a formal system but because human beings, embedded in the world, have developed mathematical intuitions — tacit sensibilities for structure — that track features of reality that no explicit algorithm directed them to track (Wigner, 1960).

Read through the lens of the *Attentional Arbitrage* framework, Wigner's argument is a statement about why the long position in the attentional arbitrage cannot be replicated by machines. The "unreasonable" effectiveness of human mathematical intuition is precisely the effectiveness of tacit knowing — the subsidiary awareness that a trained human brings to open problems, which enables recognition of structure that no explicit specification could have anticipated. This is the cognitive substrate that Polanyi named, that Wigner demonstrated, and that machine capability expansion cannot absorb.

**Wigner's Friend (1961)** is the thought experiment in which Wigner asks: if his friend inside a laboratory performs a quantum measurement, and Wigner outside does not know the result, is the friend in a superposition of "has measured up" and "has measured down" from Wigner's perspective? The experiment is primarily a contribution to the foundations of quantum mechanics, and has generated a substantial recent literature on observer-dependent reality and the measurement problem (Del Santo, Manzano, & Brukner, 2024; *Quantum Zeitgeist*, 2025). But its philosophical significance extends beyond physics. Wigner's Friend raises the question of whether the knowing subject can be treated as an object in a formal description — whether observation can be fully externalized — in terms that directly parallel Polanyi's claim that tacit knowledge cannot be made fully explicit. The observer who collapses a wave function is, for Wigner, doing something that cannot be captured in the formal structure of quantum mechanics. The practitioner who makes an expert judgment is, for Polanyi, doing something that cannot be captured in the formal structure of any explicit rule system. These are the same claim, posed in different registers.

## IV. John von Neumann: The Architecture of What Machines Are and Are Not

Von Neumann's intellectual range was so vast that any summary risks caricature. In the decade between 1932 and 1944, he produced the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics (*Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik*, 1932), invented the minimax theorem and co-founded game theory (*Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*, with Morgenstern, 1944), and contributed foundational work in functional analysis, ergodic theory, and the mathematics of continuous geometry. In the decade that followed, he designed the stored-program computer architecture that bears his name (the First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC, 1945), built the MANIAC computer, contributed to the Manhattan Project, and developed the theory of cellular automata and self-reproducing machines (*Theory of Self-Reproducing Automata*, 1966, posthumous).

Two of these contributions define the frame within which the *Attentional Arbitrage* argument operates.

**The von Neumann architecture** — the stored-program computer in which instructions and data share the same memory and the processor operates by fetching and executing one instruction at a time — defines the computational substrate on which all classical AI systems run. Every AI model that saturates a domain on the jagged frontier does so on a von Neumann machine. The architecture is exquisitely suited to the manipulation of explicit, codified information at high speed and scale. What von Neumann himself understood is that this architecture is fundamentally different from the architecture of biological brains: in his posthumously published *The Computer and the Brain* (1958), he noted that the brain does not operate on the serial, formal-symbol-manipulation principles of the stored-program computer, and that the brain's "language" — to the extent it has one — may not be the kind of formal language that the mathematical theory of automata describes. Von Neumann did not resolve this question before his death in 1957. He posed it. It is the same question Polanyi posed from the philosophy side.

**The theory of self-reproducing automata** — von Neumann's proof that a sufficiently complex automaton could contain a complete description of itself and use that description to construct copies — established that self-reproduction does not require a vital force, only sufficient complexity. Recent scholarship has returned to this work to ask what its limits imply for AI (Sayama et al., 2024). Von Neumann's result establishes the conditions under which formal description is sufficient for replication. What it does not show — and what the self-reproducing automaton framework cannot show — is that formal description is sufficient for the acquisition of the kind of knowledge that requires a history, an apprenticeship, and a body. The automaton reproduces its description. It cannot reproduce its tacit knowing, because it has none.

**Game theory** (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944) is the direct antecedent of the economic frameworks the *Attentional Arbitrage* companion documents deploy. The equilibrium concepts that allow Ide (2026) to formalize the intergenerational transmission trap — the demonstration that competitive equilibria can produce socially excessive automation because no agent internalizes the social cost of tacit knowledge erosion — are built on the foundations that von Neumann and Morgenstern laid. The market that misprices cognitive capital does so through mechanisms that von Neumann's mathematical framework makes precise, even as Polanyi's philosophical framework names what is being mispriced.

## V. The Dialogue That Ran for a Decade: Polanyi, Wigner, and the Measurement Problem as Epistemological Limit

The most important connection in this lineage is the one least known outside the history and philosophy of science: the sustained intellectual correspondence between Polanyi and Wigner from 1961 to 1971, reconstructed and analyzed in *Physics in Perspective* (Springer, 2011) by historian of science Babak Ashrafian Bonab.

Wigner had, in "Remarks on the Mind-Body Question" (1961), argued that the collapse of the quantum wave function requires a conscious observer — that the formal apparatus of quantum mechanics cannot be closed without introducing an element that physics cannot itself describe. This was his philosophical sharpening of the Wigner's Friend puzzle. Polanyi read it as a confirmation of the tacit dimension: that knowing cannot be fully externalized into any formal structure, and that quantum mechanics had arrived, from within physics, at the same conclusion that *Personal Knowledge* had argued from the philosophy of science.

Their correspondence, however, revealed a deep disagreement. Wigner was ultimately committed to the idea that consciousness plays a physically special role — that the mind is the site at which the indeterminacy of quantum reality is resolved. Polanyi rejected this as an unnecessary mystification: the tacit dimension was an epistemological claim, not an ontological one about the special causal power of minds. What mattered was not that consciousness collapses wave functions but that knowledge cannot be fully articulated, and that this is a feature of knowing rather than a defect of knowers.

The disagreement was never resolved. But the dialogue itself — a physicist and a philosopher, both Hungarian, both formed by the same Bildung, circling the same question from different directions — is the intellectual precursor to the claim that animates the *Attentional Arbitrage* framework: that there is a form of knowing whose structure is such that no formal system can hold it. Polanyi named it tacit. Wigner demonstrated its presence at the frontier of physics. Von Neumann's architecture embodies the formal system that cannot hold it. The framework inherits all three.

## VI. The Budapest Circle and the Epistemology of Concentrated Genius

Wigner, von Neumann, and their contemporaries — Leo Szilard, Edward Teller, Theodore von Kármán, Dennis Gabor, John Harsányi, Georg von Békésy — formed what physicists came to call the "Martians": Hungarian Jewish intellectuals, products of the same Budapest educational culture, whose disproportionate contribution to twentieth-century science prompted the joking explanation that they must have arrived from another planet. A less whimsical explanation is available, and it bears directly on the *Attentional Arbitrage* framework's arguments about the intergenerational transmission of tacit knowledge.

The Fasori Gymnasium, and the broader Hungarian scientific-intellectual culture of the early twentieth century, constituted precisely the kind of community of practice through which collective tacit knowledge is transmitted: a dense network of mutual recognition, high-standard peer engagement, open-problem culture (KoMaL), and personal mentorship by teachers of exceptional caliber who treated their most capable students as junior participants in a shared intellectual life rather than as examinees to be ranked. Rátz gave von Neumann private lessons and introduced him to university mathematicians while he was still a gymnasium student. Polanyi established personal relationships with his research assistants that lasted lifetimes — he loaned Wigner money, advised him on personal matters, and sustained a correspondence with him for over four decades. Abraham Pais's judgment that Polanyi "decisively marked Wigner's thinking, not just about physics, but also about philosophy and politics" (Springer, 2016) is not a biographical footnote. It is a description of exactly the mechanism that Ide (2026) and Shen and Tamkin (2026) are now formalizing as the mechanism under threat from AI-driven entry-level automation.

The Martians were not produced by a system optimized for legible outcomes. They were produced by a system optimized for the conditions under which tacit knowing forms: personal relationships, high-challenge open problems, autonomy-supportive mentorship, and a community of practice whose collective intelligence exceeded the sum of its parts. The same system that produced them — the Fasori Gymnasium — closed in 1952 under the pressures of mid-century totalitarianism. What was lost when it closed was not a building or a curriculum. It was a community of practice whose collective tacit knowledge could not be written down and reconstituted elsewhere. This is Ide's intergenerational trap made historical.

## VII. What the Lineage Means for the Attentional Arbitrage Framework

The *Attentional Arbitrage* README opens with Polanyi's "We can know more than we can tell." It did not note that the author of that line was the doctoral supervisor of a Nobel laureate; that the laureate cited his supervisor in the most famous philosophical paper in twentieth-century physics; that the laureate and his supervisor sustained a decade-long dialogue on exactly the question of what formal systems cannot capture; and that both were formed by the same Budapest educational culture that also produced the architect of the stored-program computer whose limits their dialogue was attempting to specify.

This is not an interesting genealogical coincidence. It is a structural argument. The four figures in this lineage converge on a single claim from four different angles:

Rátz, through pedagogy: the conditions under which genuine capability forms are not the conditions of competitive metric optimization. They are the conditions of open-problem culture, personal mentorship, and autonomous engagement. What he created at Fasori is the opposite of what the prestige competition structure creates.

Polanyi, through philosophy of knowledge: the deepest form of knowing — the form on which all explicit knowledge depends — cannot be made fully explicit without destroying itself. Any system that processes only explicit information is constitutively incapable of holding what knowing most essentially is.

Wigner, through physics: even at the foundations of the most mathematically successful science in history, the formal structure is insufficient without a knower. The "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematical intuition in physics is a demonstration of tacit knowing at work. The measurement problem is what happens when you try to close the formal system without acknowledging the knower's irreducible contribution.

Von Neumann, through architecture and mathematics: the stored-program computer is a formal symbol-manipulation system of extraordinary power. Its architecture defines what it can do. Its architecture also defines — by implication, and by the distinction von Neumann himself drew in *The Computer and the Brain* — what it cannot do. What it cannot do is what Polanyi named.

The claim that machine-resistant cognitive capabilities are structurally machine-resistant is not an empirical observation about current AI performance. It is a philosophical conclusion that these four figures, from four different disciplinary vantages, converged on over the course of the twentieth century. The *Attentional Arbitrage* framework inherits that convergence. The jagged frontier (Kapoor et al., 2026) is the empirical confirmation of the philosophical argument. The philosophical argument — Polanyi's, as developed in dialogue with Wigner, against the backdrop of von Neumann's architecture and Rátz's pedagogy — is what explains why the frontier is structured the way it is, and why it will remain structured that way regardless of how much computational power is brought to bear.
